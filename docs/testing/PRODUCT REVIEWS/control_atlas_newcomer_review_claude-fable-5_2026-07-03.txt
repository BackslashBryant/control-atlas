1. FIRST CONTACT
----------------------------------------------------------------
- What Sam thought the tool was, before clicking anything:
  The headline says "Control Atlas - The public map for federal cyber
  compliance" and the subtitle says I can "Explore public controls,
  baselines, STIGs, and compliance patterns - and generate blank
  RMF/ATO templates without uploading data or creating an account."
  I got the gist immediately: it's a free reference site that maps
  compliance stuff and makes blank templates. But three sentences in,
  I'd already hit four things I couldn't define: STIGs, RMF, ATO, and
  (in the first card) CCIs. I recognized the letters from work emails;
  I could not have explained any of them.
- Was there an obvious starting point? Invited or intimidated?
  Genuinely invited. The cards are phrased as MY questions: "Where do
  I begin?", "What does this control mean?", "How do these frameworks
  relate?", "Why trust this mapping?" That's exactly how my brain is
  phrasing things two weeks into this job. There is a clear "Start
  here" button. The footer says "Open-source. No login. Public data
  only." and "Not an official government system," which lowered the
  stakes for me - I can't break anything here. Best first impression
  of any surface in the app.

----------------------------------------------------------------
2. ON-RAMP VERDICT (BLUF)
----------------------------------------------------------------
- Can a true newcomer become productive using ONLY this app,
  no course/mentor/external docs? PARTIAL.
- The orientation layer is real: the Start questionnaire, the
  Playbooks (Purpose / When to use it / How it works / Common
  mistakes), the Glossary panel, and the Templates gallery genuinely
  taught me things I did not know, in plain sentences, with sources.
  After an hour I could explain RMF's 7 steps, what an ATO is, what a
  CCI is, and what a POA&M tracker is for - from the app alone.
  But the flagship Atlas map taught me almost nothing: clicking a
  node does nothing visible despite the copy promising it will, every
  relationship is the same raw "provides_context_for" label with the
  same copy-pasted rationale, so I never learned WHY two things are
  connected. The control record's "WHAT THIS IS" box is raw catalog
  text with holes in the sentences, not plain language, so I still
  don't know what someone actually DOES for AC-2. And the first
  comparison I tried (SP 800-53 vs SP 800-171) returned "No public
  connections found," which made me stop trusting the data right when
  the app had earned my trust. Sam can get oriented here; Sam cannot
  yet get competent here.

7. LEARNING LOG
----------------------------------------------------------------
1. Landing footer taught me the stakes are low: open-source, no
   login, public data only, not an official system.
2. Start helper text taught me the three questions that frame an
   authorization: what the system is, how sensitive the data is,
   who operates it.
3. Start results banner taught me the difference between a
   reference recommendation and a compliance determination.
4. Start results Explore card taught me DoD systems still trace to
   NIST controls even when STIGs drive technical checks - my first
   mental model of how STIGs and NIST relate.
5. RMF playbook taught me RMF is a 7-step lifecycle (Prepare,
   Categorize, Select, Implement, Assess, Authorize, Monitor) and
   that treating it as one-time paperwork is the classic mistake.
6. RMF playbook "Common mistakes" taught me not to wait until the
   system is built to start control selection.
7. Glossary taught me ATO = a senior official's formal decision to
   accept risk and authorize operation (source: SP 800-37).
8. Glossary taught me CCI = a standard identifier that breaks
   high-level controls into granular technical requirements
   (source: DISA).
9. Compare's STIG card taught me the chain: STIG/SRG items link
   through CCIs to NIST controls.
10. Compare results table taught me mappings have trust levels and
    that "Official link = published mapping, Inferred link =
    candidate mapping that still needs review" - my first concept
    of mapping provenance.
11. Templates gallery taught me the artifact vocabulary of an
    authorization package: SSP (system security plan outline),
    POA&M (tracker for open weaknesses and remediation dates),
    evidence matrices, assessment planning, continuous-monitoring
    calendar.
12. Inheritance Worksheet description taught me inheritance =
    controls provided by a provider versus implemented locally -
    one sentence, but it's the app's clearest statement of the
    concept.
13. AC-2 record group explainer taught me CCIs are how DISA
    implementation/assessment requirements attach to a NIST
    control.
14. Sources page taught me to check a source's maintainer, status,
    and version before trusting a mapping.
15. About page taught me exactly what the tool will not do
    (determine compliance, make authorization decisions) - which
    told me what my own job still is.

9. PAIN-POINT COVERAGE
----------------------------------------------------------------
- Acronym overload: PARTIAL. The Glossary panel and playbook chips
  are genuinely good, but they are opt-in; page copy (landing hero,
  dropdowns, cluster names, "LI-SAAS", "GRC") still assumes the
  language, and nothing is defined inline where it's used.
- Control text vs. real action: UNADDRESSED. "WHAT THIS IS" merely
  repeats the official text (with parameter holes); nowhere does
  the app translate a control into what a person actually does.
- Inheritance / common / hybrid controls: PARTIAL. The Inheritance
  Worksheet's one-line description and the Enterprise Control
  Inheritance playbook exist and point the right way, but nothing
  explains common vs. hybrid, and the concept isn't surfaced where
  inheritance data would appear.
- Control relationships: UNADDRESSED at the map level. Every edge
  is "provides_context_for" with one recycled rationale sentence;
  the Compare table is the only place a relationship is ever
  specific.
- Where do I start / where am I in the process: RESOLVED. The
  Start questionnaire plus the RMF Lifecycle playbook is a real
  on-ramp - the app's standout achievement.
- How the pieces fit: PARTIAL. The Templates gallery quietly
  teaches the artifact ecosystem, and Start results sequence the
  pieces, but the Atlas - the surface built for this exact job -
  doesn't deliver it, and no surface ties POA&M/SSP/baseline/
  tailoring into one narrative.
- Fear of getting it wrong: RESOLVED on posture, PARTIAL on data.
  Disclaimers, trust levels, and the honest About page are
  calibrated exactly right for an anxious newcomer; but the empty
  800-53-to-800-171 comparison and the identical-boilerplate
  rationales leak doubt back in.

10. TOP 3 THINGS THAT WOULD HELP SAM MOST
----------------------------------------------------------------
1. Give every control record a true plain-language layer: a "WHAT
   THIS IS" written in normal English (distinct from the official
   excerpt), visible placeholders where organization-defined
   parameters were stripped, and one line on "what teams typically
   do about it." This is the single gap between "I found AC-2" and
   "I understand AC-2," and it's the app's own stated promise
   ("Open a record to see what it means").
2. Make Atlas relationships mean something: fix (or clearly
   deliver) the click-a-node-to-see-details promise, replace
   "leads_to"/"provides_context_for" with human labels, and write
   one specific rationale per relationship type instead of one
   recycled sentence times eleven. Until then the flagship map is
   the least educational surface in the product.
3. Define terms at the point of use, not just in the panel: inline
   glossary tooltips (or the playbook-style chips) on landing copy,
   questionnaire answer options (CUI, CSP), Compare's framework
   dropdown, and cluster names (CSF, ATT&CK/D3FEND, LI-SaaS). The
   glossary content already exists - it just needs to travel to
   where the confusion happens.
